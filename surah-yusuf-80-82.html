01

Losing All Hope

Verse 80

فَلَمَّا ٱسْتَيْـَٔسُوا۟ مِنْهُ خَلَصُوا۟ نَجِيًّۭا ۖ قَالَ كَبِيرُهُمْ أَلَمْ تَعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ أَبَاكُمْ قَدْ أَخَذَ عَلَيْكُم مَّوْثِقًۭا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَمِن قَبْلُ مَا فَرَّطتُمْ فِى يُوسُفَ ۖ فَلَنْ أَبْرَحَ ٱلْأَرْضَ حَتَّىٰ يَأْذَنَ لِىٓ أَبِىٓ أَوْ يَحْكُمَ ٱللَّهُ لِى ۖ وَهُوَ خَيْرُ ٱلْحَٰكِمِينَ

"So when they had despaired of him, they secluded themselves in private consultation. The eldest of them said, 'Do you not know that your father has taken upon you an oath by Allah and [that] before you failed in [your duty to] Joseph? So I will never leave [this] land until my father permits me or Allah decides for me, and He is the best of judges.'" — Sahih International

"Fa-Lamma" and "Istay-asoo"

Turning point
  • "Fa-Lamma" means "so eventually, when…" — the brothers had tried convincing Yūsuf (AS) one way or another, but he simply would not budge.
  • "Istay-asoo" means they utterly lost hope in him.
    • Sometimes a "no" isn't an emphatic, absolute refusal — but this word conveys that they had no hope left whatsoever that Yūsuf (AS) would change his position.
02

Dawah Through Character

"Ma AathAllah" in a non-Muslim environment

Character as invitation
  • In the previous ayah, Yūsuf (AS) openly says "Ma AathAllah" ("I cling to Allah") even while operating in a non-Muslim environment — he does daʿwah simply through the way he carries himself.
    • The only time Allah is mentioned in this environment is when Yūsuf (AS) says it — which naturally inspires curiosity in people.
  • He did not invoke the law of the land, saying things like "I will not be breaking the country's laws," "How dare I defy the king," or "How dare I defy the law of the land."
  • Instead he said: "I will not be violating Allah's command, I seek refuge in Allah."
  • So the objection isn't framed as a violation against the laws, but as a violation against Allah — his morality doesn't come from the laws, it comes from Allah.
03

"Khalasoo": Seeking Seclusion

Two layers of meaning

"Khalasoo" · secluded
  • Meaning one: they physically went somewhere to be alone — a separate room, a corner of the street — because they could no longer talk openly.
    • Why not openly? Because they had become a kind of celebrity: it was now known that one of them was a thief, so people suspected all of them of being in on it.
    • Their reputation had flipped from good to bad — if seen huddled in conversation, onlookers would assume they were scheming something else.
  • Meaning two: they freed themselves from every other concern — this was the only thing left on their minds.
04

"Najiyya": The Secret Council

Secret meetings and their risk

"Najiyya" · secret council
  • Sūrah Al-Mujādilah (Ch. 58) devotes an entire section to "Najwa" (secret council).
    • Allah says not to meet secretly for sinful purposes, but rather to hold secret meetings for good and to increase one another in taqwā.
    • Allah later mentions that such secret meetings are actually from Shayṭān — meaning they carry more potential for harm than for good.
  • The word carries two possible meanings here:
    • They got together privately for the purpose of a secret meeting.
    • It could also function as a noun representing them — i.e. simply "they / them."
05

"Kabeeruhum": The Eldest Speaks

"Qala Kabeeruhum" — three possible senses

The "big one"
  • Earlier, in a previous secret meeting, they had said things like "dad loves him more, we should kill him." This time, we're only told the tail end of the conversation.
  • "The big one among them said" could mean:
    • One: the eldest among them.
    • Two: someone who deserves respect — earlier their father Yaʿqūb (AS) was described as "Shaykhan Kabeeran."
    • Three: someone who commands authority — someone who is responsible.
      • In Sūrah Al-Aḥzāb, it is said that on Judgment Day people will come to Allah saying, "We followed our leaders and our big ones, and they made us lose the path."
      • Ibrāhīm (AS), when he broke the idols, said "Go ask the big one."
  • This tells us that the one who was eldest or responsible (or both) spoke to his brothers because he knew he would be the one questioned when they returned home — not the others.

"Do you not know that your father has taken an oath by Allah"

Know vs. remember
  • What the eldest/responsible brother said is a response to things that were discussed in the meeting.
  • "Don't you know?" is more critical than "Don't you remember?"
  • Some of them in this private meeting were likely saying, "Well, we tried, but I'm not that broken up about it."
  • The other brothers weren't much concerned — but this one, the responsible one, had watched his father suffer for so long that guilt was catching up with him.
06

Echoes of the Father

Intertextuality: reading the text against itself

Method note
  • Intertextuality is when a passage later in a text is connected to something stated earlier in the same text.
  • There's a lot of intertextuality in this brother's words — it's as if his father's words have begun to affect him, like water dripping on rock: a small, continuous effect that eventually wears a hole through it.
Verse 66

قَالَ لَنْ أُرْسِلَهُۥ مَعَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ تُؤْتُونِ مَوْثِقًۭا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ لَتَأْتُنَّنِى بِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَن يُحَاطَ بِكُمْ ۖ فَلَمَّآ ءَاتَوْهُ مَوْثِقَهُمْ قَالَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَا نَقُولُ وَكِيلٌۭ

"[Jacob] said, 'Never will I send him with you until you give me a promise by Allah that you will bring him [back] to me, unless you should be surrounded by enemies.' And when they had given their promise, he said, 'Allah, over what we say, is Witness.'" — Sahih International

"Mawthiqan mina Allah" — the same phrase, now on the son's tongue

"Promise of Allah"
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) said these very words in Verse 66, before they left on this trip — the same exact phrasing echoed by the father is now being echoed by the son.
  • Those words are still ringing in his own head, and now he's making his brothers' ears ring with them too.
  • Mention of the father, and also of Allah: there's not only an emotional reconnection with his father here, but also a spiritual reconnection with Allah — an echo of Yaʿqūb's (AS) own words, "Allah is Witness over what they say."
Verse 64

قَالَ هَلْ ءَامَنُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ إِلَّا كَمَآ أَمِنتُكُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَخِيهِ مِن قَبْلُ ۖ فَٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌ حَٰفِظًۭا ۖ وَهُوَ أَرْحَمُ ٱلرَّٰحِمِينَ

"He said, 'Should I entrust you with him except [under coercion] as I entrusted you with his brother before? But Allah is the best guardian, and He is the most merciful of the merciful.'" — Sahih International

"Ma Farrat-tum Fee Yoosufa" — that you failed concerning Yūsuf (AS)

Knowing = realizing
  • This can be read as, "Don't you know your transgressions — what lines you crossed with Yūsuf (AS)?"
  • He is reminding them not of Binyāmīn, but of Yūsuf (AS) from so long ago — meaning he had shelved that guilt for years but could no longer keep it there.
  • "Do you even know what you've done?" — here, knowing means realizing.
  • These words echo Yaʿqūb's (AS) own words from Verse 64.
07

The Flicker of Light

"Wa-min-qablu Ma Farrat-tum Fee Yoosufa"

Naming him directly
  • "Wa Min Qablu" means "long ago, before."
  • Their father Yaʿqūb (AS) never mentioned the name — he only said "his brother" — but here the brother speaks of Yūsuf (AS) by name.

A profound moment of coming back to light

The flicker
  • This brother is experiencing what could be called "the flicker" — a profound moment of returning toward light.
  • Sometimes people spiral into darkness for so long, going deeper and deeper — Allah compares this in Sūrah An-Nūr to someone drowning in the ocean.
    • There's less and less light the deeper one sinks — a massive wave, and above that wave, dark clouds through which the sun can't penetrate.
    • So there's a dark cloud, then wave upon wave, and the person is deep down inside it.
  • If you held your hand out in that kind of darkness, you would expect not to see it at all — so the wording should logically be "When he takes his hand out, he couldn't see it."
  • Instead the wording is "When he takes his hand out, it's almost as if he couldn't see it" — meaning there's still a possibility of light.
  • Moral: a person could be in the depths of sin, unrepentant for years, and there's still some light Allah placed inside them that refuses to give up — because that very light will testify against them on Judgment Day.
  • No matter how deep a person goes into sin, there's still hope for them — they aren't blind yet.
  • So here, the brother is finally seeing his own hand.
08

"Falan Abraha Al-Ard"

"Never will I leave this land until my father gives me permission…"

Echo of Verse 66
  • This too is an echo — Yaʿqūb (AS) said in Verse 66, "unless you bring him back to me."
  • The brother is effectively saying: "I don't know if dad will consider this a sufficient excuse — and if I can't justify it, then I'm not coming home."
  • Even though this time he has a legitimate excuse to go back, once your conscience has awakened, you become doubly cautious to ensure you're truly in the right.
  • There's a real difference between someone who just wants something to be permissible, and someone who wants to double- and triple-check that it's fully permissible before acting.

"Aw Yahkuma Allahu Lee"

Verse 67

وَقَالَ يَٰبَنِىَّ لَا تَدْخُلُوا۟ مِنۢ بَابٍۢ وَٰحِدٍۢ وَٱدْخُلُوا۟ مِنْ أَبْوَٰبٍۢ مُّتَفَرِّقَةٍۢ ۖ وَمَآ أُغْنِى عَنكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ مِن شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنِ ٱلْحُكْمُ إِلَّا لِلَّهِ ۖ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ ۖ وَعَلَيْهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ ٱلْمُتَوَكِّلُونَ

"And he said, 'O my sons, do not enter from one gate but enter from different gates; and I cannot avail you against [the decree of] Allah at all. The decision is only for Allah; upon Him I have relied, and upon Him let those who would rely [indeed] rely.'" — Sahih International

"Or Allah decides for me" — "And He is the best of judges"

Echo of Verse 67
  • "Aw Yahkuma Allahu lee" means Allah decrees something — perhaps miraculously Binyāmīn is released, or perhaps Allah decides the brother will die there.
  • "And He is the best of judges" — he's leaving the situation entirely in Allah's hand, which is unique coming from this brother.
  • This statement is again an echo of what the father said in Verse 67: "and upon Him let those who would rely, rely" — and here we have the son putting his own trust in Allah.
  • When someone finds their way back to Allah, they do right by the people they've hurt, and they do right by Allah — since he had violated both.
  • Allah does not consider people simply black and white, good or evil. People can go through evil episodes — even ones lasting years — but as long as breath remains, judgment has not been passed on them.
  • There is a unique feature in the Qur'ān's language here that occurs only twice, and this ayah is one of those two occurrences.

Grammar Notes: "Ala" & "Ma"

"Akhatha Mawthiqan ... Alaikum" — a covenant that hangs over you

Preposition "Ala"
  • Ordinarily, "taking a promise" (Akhatha Mawthiqan) in Arabic comes with the preposition "Minn" ("from") — e.g. "Akhathna Minn Bani Israel Meethaqahum."
  • Here, however, the preposition used is "Ala" ("Alaikum") — a usage found only once elsewhere in the Qur'ān, in Sūrah Al-Aʿrāf.
  • Disclaimer noted in the source: this particular observation is not addressed in any tafsīr and is based only on studying the preposition "Ala."
  • Instead of "father took a covenant from you," the phrase reads more like "father took a covenant over you" — meaning it's hanging over your heads, something binding, not a promise that was simply made and then disappeared. It's like a cloud over you — an obligation.
  • This may be Allah directing special attention to this brother's awakening: when someone truly wakes up, they realize the promise isn't just something they once said to Allah — it's something that hangs over them, and they remain responsible for it even if they act otherwise.

Three grammatical readings of "Ma Farrat-tum"

The particle "Ma"
  • Reading one (Ālūsī's view): the particle "Ma" ("that / what") is grammatically extra ("zāʾid") and isn't translated on its own.
    • Translation: "Didn't you know that your father took a sacred promise from you by Allah that hangs over you — not to mention how much wrong you did to Yūsuf (AS) so long ago?"
    • The word "what" is left untranslated — but the presence of "Ma" signals that "Farrat-tum" carries an exclamatory force, as if there is yelling behind it.
  • Reading two: it could branch two ways under "Don't you know…" — (a) what promise you made to father, or (b) what you did to Yūsuf (AS).
  • Reading three: "Don't you know that your father took a sacred promise from you by Allah that hangs over you…" as one sentence, followed by a new sentence: "…and by the way, so long ago, what did you do with Yūsuf (AS)?"
  • In summary:
    • 1st meaning combines both into a single crime — wrong done to the father and wrong done to Yūsuf (AS).
    • 2nd meaning keeps both separate: you're committing a crime now, and long ago you'd already done this to Yūsuf (AS).
    • 3rd meaning reminds them of only the promise to the father, then adds a fresh sentence about Yūsuf (AS).

"Baraah": Standing Firm

The root behind "I will never leave"

"Baraah" / "Bareeha"
  • "Bareeha" or "Baraah" in Arabic describes soil or sand that the winds blow far and wide — something that moves far and wide is called "Baraah." (Even the sun's changing position was described this way in ancient Arabic.)
  • From this comes "La Abrahu" — "I will not shift from my place," a phrase also used in Sūrah Al-Kahf (in the story of the meeting of the two seas), meaning "I will stand my ground."
  • So here the brother is saying he will not budge from his position until his father gives him permission.
  • "Until father gives me permission" means until he explicitly gives permission — the brothers could claim, "Father said if you're surrounded, then it's fine," but he insists it must be given specifically to him, not folded into the general conversation they'd had.
  • Earlier they said "he has a father…"; now this brother says "my father."
  • "Until my father permits me" could also mean "until my father has told me this himself, directly" — because he knows his brothers are liars too.

"Irjiʿoo": Go Back and Be Honest

Verse 81

ٱرْجِعُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَبِيكُمْ فَقُولُوا۟ يَـٰٓأَبَانَآ إِنَّ ٱبْنَكَ سَرَقَ وَمَا شَهِدْنَآ إِلَّا بِمَا عَلِمْنَا وَمَا كُنَّا لِلْغَيْبِ حَٰفِظِينَ

"Return to your father and say, 'O our father, indeed your son has stolen, and we did not testify except to what we knew. And we were not witnesses of the unseen.'" — Sahih International

Instructions to the brothers

"Irjiʿoo" · Go back
  • In response to the eldest brother saying he will not go back until his father gives him permission, it's almost as if the others ask, "Then what are we going to do?" — so he gives them instructions.
  • "Go back to your father" could mean either:
    • physically returning to their father, or
    • making things right — going back to him metaphorically.
  • Before, they lied — this time, go back and be honest.
13

"Your Son Has Stolen"

Still throwing him under the bus

Not black and white
  • "O our father, there is no doubt about this, your son has stolen" — again, they don't say "our brother has stolen," they say "your son" has stolen, meaning: this isn't our fault, he did this.
  • Even though this brother has had a flicker of light, he's still throwing his own brother under the bus here — a reminder that the Qur'ān doesn't depict people in strict black and white.
14

Witnesses of the Unseen

"And we did not testify except to what we knew"

Doubt creeping in
  • All they actually know is that the cup was found in his bag, and that everyone saw it happen.
  • They don't know how the cup ended up there, when it was placed, when — or even whether — he stole it at all.
  • They opened by saying "there's no doubt about it, your son has stolen," but now it sounds as if doubt has crept back in, likely because their father isn't buying the story.

"And we were not witnesses of the unseen"

"Ghayb" · unseen
  • They didn't know he was going to steal, so how could they be expected to guard against it? They guarded him against thieves and robbers and everything else — but not against the possibility that he himself would be the thief.
  • One commentator, Zajjāj, suggests "Ghayb" could also mean "night time" here — meaning they couldn't guard against what happens at night; perhaps he stole while they were all asleep.

"Hafitheen": Echo of the Vow

Verse 63

فَلَمَّا رَجَعُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَبِيهِمْ قَالُوا۟ يَـٰٓأَبَانَا مُنِعَ مِنَّا ٱلْكَيْلُ فَأَرْسِلْ مَعَنَآ أَخَانَا نَكْتَلْ وَإِنَّا لَهُۥ لَحَٰفِظُونَ

"So when they returned to their father, they said, 'O our father, [further] measure has been denied to us, so send with us our brother [that] we will be given measure. And indeed, we will be his guardians.'" — Sahih International

"Guardian" — the very same word, again

"Hafitheen"
  • "Hafitheen" means guardian.
  • Earlier, in Verse 63, they promised, "And indeed, we will be guardians over him."
  • Now, in Verse 81, they use that same word again — an ironic echo, given how that earlier guardianship ended.

"Ask the City... We Are Truthful"

Verse 82

وَسْـَٔلِ ٱلْقَرْيَةَ ٱلَّتِى كُنَّا فِيهَا وَٱلْعِيرَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَقْبَلْنَا فِيهَا ۖ وَإِنَّا لَصَٰدِقُونَ

"And ask the city in which we were and the caravan in which we came — and indeed, we are truthful." — Sahih International

Whose words are these — the brother's, or a scene switch?

Two readings
  • This ayah can be understood in two ways:
    • as a continuation of the same brother's quote from the previous ayah, still speaking within the secret council; or
    • as a switch of scene, with the brothers now actually saying this to their father.

"Ask the city" — appealing to outside witnesses

"Aqbalna Feeha"
  • "And ask the city" could mean "ask the people" — or it could be dramatic flourish, as if to say "ask the city, ask the walls, they will testify."
  • "Aqbalna Feeha" refers to the roughly hundred other people who were part of the caravan — "ask them."
    • This could mean either the caravan that turned back when the guards called them out, or the caravan that returned together with them.
  • They say this because they know their own testimony isn't enough — their father doesn't trust them anymore.
  • The elder brother is telling them plainly that they have no credibility left — anything they say now must be corroborated by the city or by the people of the caravan.
Verse 17 (earlier in the sūrah)

"But you would not believe us, even if we were truthful."

"Wa-Inna Lasadiqoon" — "And indeed, we are truthful"

Echo of Verse 17
  • Why say this now? Earlier in the sūrah they had said, "But you would not believe us, even if we were truthful" (Verse 17).
  • It's as if to say: "We lied before — but this time, we really are telling the truth."
