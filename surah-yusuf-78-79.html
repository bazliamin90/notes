"Al-ʿAzeez": A New Title

Verse 78

قَالُوا۟ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْعَزِيزُ إِنَّ لَهُۥٓ أَبًۭا شَيْخًۭا كَبِيرًۭا فَخُذْ أَحَدَنَا مَكَانَهُۥٓ ۖ إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"They said, 'O ʿAzeez, indeed he has a father, an old man, so take one of us in his place. Indeed, we see you as a doer of good.'" — Sahih International

From Potiphar's title to Yūsuf's

"Al-ʿAzeez"
  • The brothers address Yūsuf (AS) with the title "Al-ʿAzeez", meaning "the Minister."
  • This is the very title that was previously used for the minister Yūsuf (AS) once worked for — Potiphar.
  • One reading: now that Yūsuf (AS) has been exonerated and the minister's cover-up exposed, Potiphar has effectively been replaced by the very man who used to work for him as a slave.
  • Another reading: Yūsuf (AS) may now hold an even higher rank than Potiphar did, since he has authority to move and settle anywhere in the land — a detail mentioned in an earlier verse.
  • The root of "ʿAzeez" is "ʿIzza." When soil or dirt is packed so firmly that it won't move, that firmness is described with this root — a picture of solidity and strength.
  • "Al-ʿAzeez" is also one of Allah's names, usually translated as "the Almighty."
  • The concept connects to someone who is principled and holds power, yet never budges from that position — like a statue fixed in place, a firm foundation, or a stable government.
02

ʿIzza vs. Dhillah

Power, dignity, and genuine respect

Word study
  • In the Qur'ān, "Al-ʿAzeez" functions as a word of respect and stands as the opposite of "Dhillah."
    • "Dhillah" → to be powerless and humiliated.
    • Its opposite, "ʿIzza," → to have power and dignity.
  • Two common ways this idea of "Al-ʿAzeez" shows up:
    • Someone who demands respect because of the power they hold — for example, a judge.
    • Someone who has power but receives only artificial, grudging respect — for example, a student who listens to a teacher out of obligation, not admiration.
  • "ʿIzza" more precisely describes a case where someone genuinely has respect for another person.
  • So when the brothers use this title for Yūsuf (AS), they are already conceding how much respect they hold for him and how much authority he now has.
    • The underlying message: "We don't mean any disrespect, and we certainly don't have any influence over your decision."
03

The ʿĪsā (AS) Connection

A word that removes any claim to authority

Cross-reference
  • This is the same word that ʿĪsā (AS) / Jesus is described as using when he speaks on behalf of the people who committed shirk.
  • By using it, he pleads for them while making clear he has no say whatsoever in Allah's authority or decision.
  • This detail carries theological weight: the Christian creed holds that Jesus has a say in salvation.
  • By choosing this particular name of Allah, he uses language that eliminates any possibility that he has any say in salvation.
04

"Shaykhan Kabeeran": The Old Father

A description meant to tug at compassion

"Shaykhan Kabeeran" · very old
  • The phrase "Shaykhan Kabeeran" describes the father as very old.
  • "Kabeeran" carries two possible senses:
    • He is very, very old in age.
    • It can also connect to "Kareem" — meaning he holds a lot of respect in people's eyes, a dignified and respectable person, a good man.
  • The brothers' underlying message: "We don't care about him — theft runs in his family, as we already said. But he has a father who would be deeply hurt…"
05

"He Has a Father" (Lahu)

Notice what they didn't say

"Lahu" · has
  • The brothers say "he has a father" — not "we have a father."
  • Using "he" instead of "we" illustrates that the father has a uniquely special relationship with this particular son.
  • The same emphasis appeared earlier, when they said they would have to slip Binyāmīn away from his father.
  • The word "lahu" means "has," and can be read as "he's got a father who is entirely for him."
  • In effect: the father is completely devoted to this son — he belongs to the father, and the father belongs to him.
  • The father is described as totally attached and unable to bear letting this son leave his side.

Al-Muḥsinīn: Echo of Verse 36

Verse 36 (first occurrence)

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ ٱلسِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ ۖ ... إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"...Inform us of its interpretation; indeed, we see you to be of those who do good." — Sahih International

Verse 56

وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَكَّنَّا لِيُوسُفَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ... وَلَا نُضِيعُ أَجْرَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"...and We do not allow to be lost the reward of those who do good." — Sahih International

"Inna Naraka Mina Al-Muhsineen" — deliberate repetition

"Muḥsin"
  • This is the same father the brothers earlier called "in error," and whom they will later go on to yell at.
  • This is a case of saying exactly what the other person wants to hear.
  • They know Yūsuf (AS) is compassionate toward the needy and poor and has a soft side, so they try to appeal directly to that.
  • "Inna Naraka Mina Al-Muhsineen" — "Indeed, we see you as someone who does good."
  • "Muhsin" can carry several layered meanings here:
    • You have already done a lot of good for us, and we've seen it — so please grant us this one more kindness.
    • We have seen that you have done good for the entire country and that you are a person of mercy and care.
    • We see that you not only follow the law but are also a merciful judge.
  • This is the second time this exact phrase is used — the first was in Verse 36, spoken by the prisoners asking him to interpret their dreams.
  • This is a deliberate echo, not a coincidence — the Qur'ān repeats it intentionally.
    • The first time, he was in the lowest position anyone could be.
    • The second time, he is in the highest position.
  • In both scenarios, anyone who observes him recognizes that he does good.
  • This may also echo Verse 56, where Allah uses the same word: "And We do not allow to be lost the reward of those who do good."
  • Moral: character rooted in "Ihsan" (from "Muhsin") is not a product of circumstances.
    • When someone lives by "Ihsan" toward Allah, changing circumstances — difficulty or ease — does not change that character.
  • Such people take every opportunity to do good; their "Ihsan" remains constant.
07

Why Yaʿqūb (AS) Shows Special Love

Bible's answer

Shared lineage

Yūsuf (AS) and Binyāmīn come from the same mother. Since the father loved her most, the explanation goes, he loves her children more — but this is not the Qur'ān's answer.

Qur'ān's answer

Character, not lineage

A father shows special love and consideration for a child who demonstrates special promise and character — and the same principle applies in reverse, for harshness.

Seeking Refuge in Allah

Verse 79

قَالَ مَعَاذَ ٱللَّهِ أَن نَّأْخُذَ إِلَّا مَن وَجَدْنَا مَتَـٰعَنَا عِندَهُۥٓ إِنَّآ إِذًۭا لَّظَـٰلِمُونَ

"He said, '[I seek] the refuge of Allah, [to prevent] that we take except him with whom we found our possession. Indeed, we would then be unjust.'" — Sahih International

Refuge sought from a position of strength

"Maʿādha Allāhi"
  • Some part of the brothers genuinely does care about their father — they are afraid of being called liars again.
  • "Ma-AAatha Allahi" means "I cling to Allah for refuge."
  • Yūsuf (AS) also used this exact phrase earlier, when the minister's wife tried to seduce him.
  • What is he seeking refuge from here? From the evil potential within his own power — from becoming an unfair judge.
  • Normally, people seek Allah's protection when they are weak — but here Yūsuf (AS) seeks protection from Allah precisely when he is strong.
09

"We Will Take": The Royal We

Four layers behind a single pronoun

"Na Khutha" · We take
  • "That we would take anybody except the one we found our goods with."
  • By seeking Allah's refuge from arresting the wrong person for the wrong crime, Yūsuf (AS) carefully avoids the words "arrest" or "thief."
  • He instead says "except the one we found our goods with" — a phrase that doesn't necessarily accuse anyone of theft, conveying the meaning without labelling him a thief.
  • Why the word "We"? Four possible layers:
    • One: Royalty traditionally speaks as "We," reflecting his position of authority — even the cup he drinks from is not his personal property but government property.
    • Two: A side implication that he speaks on behalf of everyone — "We as Egypt are speaking."
    • Three: He includes everyone in "We abide by the rules and regulations here" — this decision is bigger than himself alone.
    • Four: "We" may mean "me and my brother" (Binyāmīn) — hinting that this is their shared plan, communicated indirectly rather than in direct words.
10

"Mataʿanā": Wordplay on Possessions

Three meanings folded into one word

"Mata'ana" · Possessions
  • First meaning: goods, possessions.
  • Second meaning: in Arabic the word also carries a sense of enjoyment — Yūsuf (AS) found enjoyment in being with his brother, so on this reading he's saying they won't let go of someone they found so much enjoyment with, i.e. his brother.
  • Third meaning: something that can be utilized — meaning they won't let go of anyone except the one they intend to put to use for their plan, since the plan is bound up with him.
  • Altogether, this is a deliberate play on words layered into a single term.

A matter of impression and authority

Leadership note
  • Yūsuf (AS) is known for being generous. If he keeps being lenient, people may stop taking him seriously.
  • He has to demonstrate his authority now, because people are watching him closely.
  • This mirrors the position of a judge — if a judge is too lenient, people stop following the rules.

"Indeed, We Would Be Unjust"

Verse 23 (first occurrence)

... قَالَ مَعَاذَ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَاىَ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ

"He said, 'I seek the refuge of Allah. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed.'" — Sahih International

"Inna ithan lathalimoon" — a second echo

Justice
  • "Inna ithan lathalimoon" means "Indeed, we would then be unjust."
  • If preferential treatment were given, or someone else's punishment placed on the wrong person, that would itself be an injustice.
  • This echoes an earlier moment in the sūrah: the first time Yūsuf (AS) said "Ma-AAatha Allah" ("I seek refuge in Allah") was in Verse 23, where he followed it with "Innahu la yuflihu aththalimoon" ("Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed").
  • Here in Verse 79, he follows the same refuge-seeking phrase with similar words once again.
  • A subtle connection is being established between two ideas: seeking Allah's protection, and being a wrongdoer.
    • The connection: he seeks Allah's protection not only from evil in general, but because failing to do so would place him among the wrongdoers himself.
  • In essence, he gives a principled response — he will not do them wrong, despite their pleading.

Power Reversed: Echo of Verse 8

Verse 8 (beginning of the story)

إِذْ قَالُوا۟ لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰٓ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ إِنَّ أَبَانَا لَفِى ضَلَـٰلٍۢ مُّبِينٍ

"When they said, 'Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we, while we are a clan. Indeed, our father is in clear error.'" — Sahih International

From "Nahnu usba" to "We will take"

Full-circle contrast
  • At the very beginning of the story, in Verse 8, the brothers described themselves as "Nahnu usba" — "while we are a powerful band / group / huge number."
  • Now, it is Yūsuf (AS) who says "We will take…" — speaking from a position of authority, while it is the brothers who are isolated and powerless.
  • The situation has been completely flipped.
  • This closing contrast displays a deliberate shift of power across the sūrah, from the brothers' collective strength at the start to Yūsuf's singular authority by the end.
