01

Who Was He

A late arrival to Islam, a companion without a story told of him

Ṣaḥābī
  • He's discussed right after Nuʿaymān because their stories are similar — repeated struggle, repeated forgiveness.
  • His conversion to Islam came very late — possibly even after the Prophet ﷺ's death, or possibly just before.
  • Scholars disagree on his actual name: Mālik ibn Ḥabīb or ʿAbdullāh ibn Ḥabīb. His kunya (nickname) was Abu Mihjan.
  • He belonged to the tribe of Thaqīf (hence "ath-Thaqafī") — the same tribe covered in an earlier series about late converts tied to the story of Ṭāʾif.
  • Most historians (Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr, Ibn al-Athīr) say he accepted Islam when the rest of Thaqīf did, in Ramadan of the ninth year of the Hijrah, during the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime.
  • He is technically considered a Ṣaḥābī (companion) since he had "ṣuḥbah" (companionship status), but there are no recorded direct interactions between him and the Prophet ﷺ, due to how late he converted.
02

Character & Flaw

Every box checked, but one

5 traits
  • Hero of the Jāhiliyyah — known before Islam as one of the truly courageous men of his time.
  • A skilled horseman — a talent that would later save an entire battle.
  • An eloquent poet — his verses survive to this day, carrying both his grief and his resolve.
  • Wealthy and generous — financially well-off, and known for spending freely on others.
  • His weakness — al-khamr (alcohol) — despite all these strengths, this was his glaring weakness. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr narrates that no punisher's punishment and no blamer's blame could ever restrain him from it.
03

The Real Battlefield

How can a man be fearless in war, and defeated by himself?

  • The speaker raises the question: how can someone be so strong and brave in battle, yet fail so badly in a personal struggle?
  • This leads to the concept of jihād al-nafs / mujāhadat al-nafs (struggle against the self) being the ultimate battleground — greater than any external battle.
  • The idea: if you can conquer yourself, you can conquer anybody else.
Jihād al-nafs is the ultimate battleground. A person who is able to overcome themselves will be able to overcome anybody else.
04

Used, Not Discarded

Abu Bakr's view, and continued accountability

Repeated ḥadd
  • Despite his ongoing struggle with alcohol, Abu Bakr as-Ṣiddīq (RA) still made use of Abu Mihjan in battle.
  • This shows people struggling with personal sins aren't necessarily disqualified from serving the community — a person doesn't have to be "sin-free" to contribute.
  • However, accountability still applied: ʿUmar (RA) carried out the ḥadd punishment (flogging) for alcohol on him seven times, with one report saying eight times.
  • This mirrors Nuʿaymān's story of repeated relapse and repeated punishment.

05

Exile & Flight

Banished — and running toward battle, not from it

Al-Qādisiyyah
  • Eventually, ʿUmar (RA) exiled Abu Mihjan to an island in the sea because of his repeated offenses.
  • ʿUmar sent a man to accompany and guard him.
  • Abu Mihjan escaped from that man and fled, catching up with Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ (RA) in Persia, right as the Battle of al-Qādisiyyah — one of the most consequential battles against the Persian Empire — was about to take place.
  • Abu Mihjan, being a man who lived for the battlefield, begged Saʿd to let him fight rather than remain in exile.
  • However, ʿUmar had specifically written to Saʿd instructing him to restrain Abu Mihjan because of his repeated relapses.
  • So Saʿd chained and locked him up instead, telling him he'd deal with him later, since there was an urgent battle to focus on.
  • Abu Mihjan was also flogged again for the alcohol before being left in restraints.
06

Poetry in Chains

Grief in restraint

What sorrow is it that the horses are driven onward by their spears, while I am here in this pathetic state, bound in my chains?
Abu Mihjan, reciting in his restraints
  • While chained, he recited poetry lamenting his state — grieving that the horses and warriors were riding out to battle while he was left bound and helpless.
  • He wept, realizing his sin had cost him a share in a meaningful moment with the Prophet's companions, and that his personal downfall was hurting his community, who needed him.
07

The Plea

His plea to Saʿd's wife, and his promise

Tawbah
  • He pleaded with Saʿd's wife to release him.
  • He promised that if Allah allowed him to survive the battle (not be killed as a martyr), he would return and voluntarily put himself back in chains — with no one needing to know he'd left.
  • He framed this temporary release as his tawbah (repentance) — a chance to fight for Allah's sake.
  • He noted: if he died as a martyr, the household would be rid of him anyway; if he survived, he'd return to captivity without causing trouble, since Saʿd would come home and find him chained regardless.
  • Saʿd's wife believed his sincerity and let him go.
If I am killed, then you're rid of me anyway. And if I don't die, I'll come back and put myself in the chains, and you won't be in trouble — because Saʿd will come home and find me right there.
08

The Battle

A masked rider appears from nowhere

Al-Balqā'
  • He took one of Saʿd's horses, known as al-Balqā'.
  • He wrapped and covered his face (masked) and appeared right as the Battle of Qādisiyyah began.
  • He fought fiercely with his spear, cutting through enemy lines left and right.
  • At the end, people searched for the masked rider on al-Balqā', but he had disappeared.
"He must be an angel — innahu malak."
Some of the Ṣaḥāba, watching him disappear
  • Some of the Ṣaḥāba speculated he must have been an angel ("malak"), since he appeared from nowhere, fought decisively, and vanished — drawing a comparison to similar accounts from the Battle of Badr.
  • Saʿd (RA), however, recognized both his own horse and Abu Mihjan's distinctive fighting style.
  • Saʿd couldn't reconcile this because he believed Abu Mihjan was still chained back at the camp.
09

Return & Release

His return to captivity, and Saʿd's discovery

  • After the battle, Abu Mihjan went back to Saʿd's wife and asked to be put back in his chains, as promised.
  • He remained in chains until Saʿd returned.
  • Saʿd's wife told him the full story upon his return.
  • Saʿd was deeply moved and emotional.
  • He went to Abu Mihjan and released him, declaring he would never strike him again, given what Allah had accomplished through his hands for the Muslims.
By Allah, after today, I will never strike a man again through whom Allah granted the Muslims what he granted them on his hands.
Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ (RA)
10

The Vow

Abu Mihjan's response and vow

  • Abu Mihjan explained that he used to drink alcohol, then undergo the ḥadd punishment, which he felt purified him of the sin — the punishment cleansing him.
  • But now, he swore by Allah that he would never drink again.
Lā wallāhi lā ashrabuhā abadan — I swear by Allah, I will never drink it again.
11

Trick of Shayṭān

"I wouldn't quit just because someone hit me"

Musannaf ʿAbdul Razzāq
  • In a narration from Musannaf ʿAbdul Razzāq, he added a deeper reflection: he used to keep drinking partly out of pride — refusing to quit just because someone else (via punishment) forced him to.
  • He didn't want to be seen as quitting "because someone hit him."
  • The speaker uses this to illustrate a trick of Shayṭān: refusing to do what's right simply because someone else told you to, rather than doing it because you personally choose to for Allah's sake.
  • Abu Mihjan affirmed he would never return to alcohol again, whether or not he was flogged — and he kept that vow for the rest of his life.
It's like someone saying: "I won't start doing what Allah commanded just because others told me to. I'll do it when I want to." That's a trick of Shayṭān.
12

Legacy

Conquered heart, continued conquest

  • His heart was described as "conquered," and he resumed serving fī sabīlillāh (in the path of Allah).
  • Scholars differ on where he died during the later Islamic conquests (al-futūḥāt) — some say Azerbaijan, others say Jurjān — but he continued fighting for the cause after breaking free of his addiction.
  • Despite his past struggle, his legacy isn't defined by being "a drunk" — instead, he's remembered as a sincere mujāhid (fighter) in Allah's path.
"I used to see khamr, alcohol, as pleasant, yet within it there are traits that can destroy the most patient and forbearing man, someone that is ḥalīm."
"I will never again in my life drink it. And I will never use it to treat someone who is sick again. It's a disease."
13

Two Lessons

Two lessons for overcoming addictive sin

  • Caveat: none of this means you shouldn't seek the specific means to cure a particular addiction — that's itself one of the ways to be sincere fī sabīlillāh. Seek help and take the practical steps needed, alongside tawbah.
  • Abu Mihjan's specific "wake-up call" was seeing himself drinking while his community fought one of Islam's most consequential battles — feeling like "a loser" for not contributing to something greater.
  • The speaker frames sin as fundamentally selfish — serving the self even when it crosses Allah's boundaries — while purpose refocuses a person toward serving others and the ummah.
Lesson 1 — From Nuʿaymān

Allah's love is bigger than your sin

Don't think your sin is too big to be forgiven, or that it's disqualified you forever. Don't despair in the mercy of Allah, or in the idea that Allah can still love you. Unlike people — who may forgive but say "I can never love you the same again" — Allah, through al-ʿAfuw, al-Ghafūr, al-Wadūd, invites the sinner back into a relationship of love, where you could still be a walī of Allah after a major sin.

Lesson 2 — From Abu Mihjan

Your purpose is bigger than your sin

A person may wake up — as with a young person reacting to the suffering in Gaza — and ask: "What am I doing with my life? Why am I spoiling myself, getting mad over silly things, sad over things that don't deserve it, while people are being slaughtered?" Purpose calls a person back to the ummah that depends on them, and to the greatness Allah put them here to unlock.

14

Closing

His sin was never the end of his story

  • The speaker closes with supplications: asking Allah to connect the listeners to His love and greatness, to forgive shortcomings, and to be pleased with the companions of the Prophet ﷺ.
  • He asks Allah to join the listeners with the Messenger ﷺ, his beloved family, and his companions in the Hereafter, and make them amongst them.
  • The talk ends with the standard Islamic closing salutations — ṣalawāt on the Prophet ﷺ and salām.
May Allah make us people who connect to His love and His greatness. May He forgive our shortcomings, and be pleased with the companions of the Prophet ﷺ. May He join us with the Messenger ﷺ, his family, and his companions, and make us amongst them. Allāhumma āmīn.
Wa ṣallallāhu ʿalā Nabiyyinā Muḥammad wa ʿalā ālihi wa ṣaḥbihi ajmaʿīn
