01

"Watawalla Anhum": Turning Away

Verse 84

وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَـٰٓأَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَٱبْيَضَّتْ عَيْنَاهُ مِنَ ٱلْحُزْنِ فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌۭ

"And he turned away from them and said, 'Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,' and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor." — Sahih International

He turns away from his sons

"Watawalla Anhum" · And He Turned Away From Them
  • Allah specifically chose to mention that Yaʿqūb (AS) turned away from his sons at this moment.
  • This carries an important lesson: when a person is overwhelmed in a conversation by those they love, it is completely okay to turn away.
  • Imam Razi held that Yaʿqūb (AS) became so upset that he physically left and went into a different room.
  • Another plausible explanation is that he stayed in the same room but "turned away" emotionally — so overwhelmed that he began talking to himself and expressing his grief, as if he had entered his own world.
  • He saw no point in continuing the conversation with his sons; he simply needed some time alone.
02

"Ya-Asafa Aala Yoosufa": Calling Out in Grief

An artistic pairing of sound

Alas, My Grief Over Yusuf
  • "Ya-Asafa Aala Yoosufa" — Alas my grief / Oh, my sorrow over Yusuf.
  • This is one of those places in the Qur'an where two similar-sounding words are used artistically together — here, "Asafa" and "Yusuf" echo one another in sound.
  • This is remarkable because the entire sūrah is about Yusuf's (AS) own sadness in his life, and about the sadness over Yusuf (AS) that lives in Yaʿqūb's (AS) life — both meanings converge in this one line.

The grammar of extreme emotion

Mutassil "ee" → Hyperbolized "aa"
  • In Arabic, when expressing possession — "my ---" — the word normally ends with the pronoun "ee" (called Mutassil).
    • E.g. "Rabbee" → My Master
    • E.g. "Rasoolee" → My Messenger
  • However, in hyperbolized, extreme forms of communication, instead of the pronoun "ee," the pronoun "aa" is used — conveying something abnormal or extreme.
  • Here, the pronoun "aa" (as in "Asafa") is used, conveying extreme emotion.
  • So Yaʿqūb (AS) is in extreme pain that he has been suppressing for a long time — and this is the moment he finally lets it out.
03

Trauma Triggers: Why Yusuf, Not Binyamin?

An old wound reopened

Psychology of a Trigger
  • A natural question: why does Yaʿqūb (AS) cry out over Yusuf (AS), and not over Binyamin or the other brother who stayed behind in Egypt — the more immediate losses?
  • In psychology, this is described as a "trigger": one traumatic event triggers the memory and pain of a previous one.
  • Ar-Razi, commenting on Yaʿqūb's (AS) psychology, explained it this way: when a person experiences an extreme sadness that replicates a previous traumatic event, it makes them re-live that old tragedy as though it had just happened.
  • It is as if Yaʿqūb (AS) is reliving the original loss of Yusuf (AS) all over again, triggered by this new calamity.

Sadness personified

The Extended Alif
  • The style of this phrase — the extended Alif at the end of "Ya-Asafa" — is used elsewhere in the Qur'an to personify abstract ideas.
  • Sadness is an idea, not a person — but by saying "Ya-Asafa," Yaʿqūb (AS) is, in effect, calling out to it as though it were a person.
  • For Yaʿqūb (AS), it is as if sadness itself is a person — and the very sadness that came with the loss of Yusuf (AS) has returned once again to haunt him.
04

Balancing Sabr and Sadness

Beautiful patience, then wailing — how do both fit together?

Reconciling the Two Ayat
  • In the previous ayah, Yaʿqūb (AS) spoke of beautiful patience ("Fa-Sabrun Jameel"). Here, in this very next ayah, he is wailing openly. How can both be reconciled?
  • This is the Qur'an's way of teaching balance between a person's emotional needs and their spiritual needs.
Spiritual Need

Sabrun Jameel

Beautiful patience — trust in Allah's plan, and refusal to lose faith even amid loss.

Emotional Need

Grief

Honest sorrow — allowing the heart to feel and express what it is actually going through.

Not a hopeless man

Every New Day, New Hope
  • Achieving this balance between spiritual and emotional needs is something every believer has to work toward in life.
  • But Yaʿqūb (AS) is not a hopeless man. It is not that after being sad day after day, he assumes tomorrow will be sad as well.
  • For him, every new day carries new hope.
05

"Wabyaddat AAaynahu": Eyes Turned White

Verse 84 (continued)

وَٱبْيَضَّتْ عَيْنَاهُ مِنَ ٱلْحُزْنِ فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌۭ

"...and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor." — Sahih International

An emotional collapse

"His Eyes Became White"
  • This phrase marks the emotional collapse of Yaʿqūb (AS) after having held onto his emotions for so long.
  • The cry was so intense that his eyes became white. Two meanings have been given for this:
    • He lost his eyesight entirely.
    • He lost the natural color of his eyes — a figure of speech that does not necessarily mean he became blind.
06

Conversion Disorder: A Medical Lens

Can grief really affect eyesight?

Body & Mind
  • From a strictly medical standpoint, this kind of sudden vision loss from crying isn't something commonly documented — yet there are many cases where all tests come back clear and a person still dies of a condition doctors cannot fully explain.
  • Besides, Yaʿqūb (AS) had been crying for many years by this point, so the underlying damage had likely been building for a long time — this one event simply triggered the visible physical impact.
  • Emotional trauma can carry real physical side effects.
  • Medically, this general phenomenon resembles what is known today as Conversion Disorder.
Conversion disorder describes a condition in which a person experiences blindness, paralysis, or other symptoms affecting the nervous system that cannot be fully explained by a physical illness or injury on its own. Symptoms typically begin suddenly, following a period of emotional or physical distress or psychological conflict. It is thought to arise from the body's reaction to a stressful event, and some research points to related neurological changes. Diagnosis relies on recognizing signs common to the disorder together with ruling out other physical causes through testing. Treatment can include psychotherapy, hypnosis, and stress-management training, along with treatment of any underlying psychological condition; the affected body part may also need physical or occupational therapy until symptoms resolve.
Paraphrased — Conversion Disorder, Rare Disease reference

What lifts the stress, lifts the symptom

The Story Confirms It
  • Based on this understanding, once the underlying stress is relieved, the affected function — such as eyesight — can return.
  • That is exactly what happens later in the story: when Yaʿqūb (AS) smells the scent of Yusuf's (AS) shirt, the stress lifts, and his eyesight returns.
07

"Fahuwa Katheem": A Lifetime of Suppression

The word "Katheem": a lid held shut

"Fahuwa Katheem" · He Was a Suppressor
  • "Fahuwa Katheem" — for he had always been someone who swallows and suppresses his feelings.
  • "Katheem" traces back to the root "Ka-Za-Ma", meaning the lid placed on something.
  • The same root also describes what happens with a camel: when it gulps something down to the point it gets stuck in its throat and blocks the air passage — that is "kazama."
  • In Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, the phrase "those who swallow anger" uses this same root, meaning those who block anger from coming out once it has become stuck inside.

Damage that built up over years

Not Overnight
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) had been holding in his trauma and emotions for a very long time.
  • Allah is telling us that his eyes did not turn white overnight — this had been building for a very long time before this moment.
08

"Asafa" vs "Huzn": Two Words, Different Weight

Two synonyms placed side by side, on purpose

"Izaj Tama Tafarraqa"
  • Both "Asafa" and "Huzn" mean sadness — but one is used at the beginning of the ayah, the other at the end.
  • "Huzn" is the more common word for sadness in the Qur'an, used roughly 40 times — a more universal expression.
  • "Asafa", by contrast, is a unique and far rarer word.
  • There is a principle in Qur'anic study called "Izaj tama tafarraqa": when two synonyms are placed together in the same context, it signals that they are actually different in meaning.
  • A reference book on this kind of near-synonym study is Mutaradifaat al-Qur'an by Abdul Rehman Gillani.

Where "Asafa" appears in the Qur'an

5 Occurrences
  • "Asafa" refers to an extreme state of sadness and anger combined, and this word occurs in the Qur'an five times.
1

Used when Musa (AS) returned after meeting with Allah and found his people worshipping the calf — described as returning "asifan."

2

Used at the beginning of Sūrah Al-Kahf, describing the Prophet (SAW): "Are you going to kill yourself over grief?"

09

Mufassiroon on the Meaning of "Asafa"

Anger, sadness, or both

Scholarly Disagreement
  • The Mufassiroon (Qur'anic exegetes) differed over the precise meaning of this word.
  • Some held it means an extreme form of anger — e.g. Allah says about Himself (referring to Pharaoh's people): "When they caused Us Asaf, We took revenge from them and drowned all of them."
  • Some held it means sadness — e.g. in a report about Ayesha (RA), she describes Abu Bakr as a man who is "Aseef," meaning he carries a great deal of sadness, at the loss of the Prophet (SAW).
  • Al-Wahidi combined both meanings, since anger is often rooted in sadness, and sadness is sometimes rooted in anger.
  • Generally, a person gets angry at someone below them (in position or status), and sad over someone above them.
  • Some scholars said it refers to a state of feeling helpless; others said it refers to anxiety.

Zamakhshari: a sadness that never fully fades

Extreme, Regret, Helplessness
  • Zamakhshari explains "Asafa" as the most extreme form of sadness, regret, and helplessness combined.
  • Along with that, it is a feeling that can persist for a very long time — comparable to how depression can continue for years — and he cites this very ayah as evidence.
  • Normally, when something continues for a long time, the body or mind gradually adjusts to it. But with "Asafa," its intensity remains even after a long time has passed.
  • A further question arises: how can Allah describe Himself as experiencing "Asaf" — how can Allah be made "sad"? Two explanations are recorded from Ibn Abbas:
    • They made Us angry.
    • They made Our believing friends sad — and Allah feels for His believers.

Closing Morals: Power & Balance of Sadness

Even prophets can be overwhelmed

Moral
  • Allah dedicated a whole ayah to Yaʿqūb's (AS) sadness — a deliberate choice, not an incidental detail.
  • Moral: people with the strongest faith — the prophets — can experience sadness so overwhelming that it can even affect their eyesight.
  • This raises a natural question: then what is the point of Iman and Sabr at all?
  • The point is that no matter how overwhelming a situation becomes, faith and patience are what allow a person to navigate through it — not to feel nothing.

Crying is not the absence of Sabr

Sabr = Trust, Not Silence
  • Crying does not mean a person lacks Sabr. Sabr means not losing trust in Allah — it does not mean suppressing every emotion into silence.
  • All of this teaches a believer the debilitating power sadness can carry, and how faith is meant to hold and carry that sadness, not erase it.
  • Faith remains beautiful even while the ugliness of sadness is being worked through.
