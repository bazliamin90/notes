01

Two Readings: Anger or Affection?

Verse 85

قَالُوا۟ تَٱللَّهِ تَفْتَؤُا۟ تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ حَتَّىٰ تَكُونَ حَرَضًا أَوْ تَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْهَـٰلِكِينَ

"They said, 'By Allah, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish.'" — Sahih International

An unusually long quotation

Why This Ayah Is Different
  • Allah does not often dedicate this much space to a direct quotation — here an entire ayah is given over to the brothers' words to their father.
  • The paraphrased sense of the line: "We swear by Allah, will you not stop mentioning Yusuf, until you make yourself sick, or you die from it?"
  • The statement can be read two very different ways, and both readings are defensible from the text.

Reading one: anger

Frustration at a Turned Back
  • In the previous ayah Yaʿqūb (AS) turned his back on his sons and cried out in grief, even letting Yusuf's (AS) name slip from his mouth again.
  • Seeing him do this could have provoked the brothers, who reacted by yelling at their father out of frustration.

Reading two: concern

The More Popular View
  • The more widely favored reading is that the sons were worried about their father — as if one of them put an arm around his shoulder and gently pleaded with him to stop dwelling on Yusuf before it destroyed his health.
  • On this reading they are trying to console him: it has been years, Yusuf is old news, and the present crisis is about Binyamin.
02

The Case for a Gentle Reading

The strongest argument: Ayah 87

"Go Seek Out Yusuf and His Brother"
  • Two ayat later, Yaʿqūb (AS) tells his sons to go search for Yusuf and his brother, and not to lose hope in Allah's mercy — since only disbelievers despair of it.
  • If the sons had just yelled at him for even mentioning Yusuf's name, it would be strange for Yaʿqūb (AS) to immediately turn around and tell them to go look for him.
  • This suggests the brothers must have approached him gently, opening the door for him to speak to them warmly about hope in Allah in return.
  • The reading of ayah 85 is therefore influenced by what comes in ayah 87 — a more positive, hope-filled exchange follows.
03

The Case for an Angry Reading

Textual clues pointing to anger

Turning Away, Repetition, Rage
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) had already turned his back on them, showing he was not comfortable with how they were behaving toward him.
  • He repeated the same words of grief he had voiced before — he is already overwhelmed, and their response can be read as anger rather than sympathy: "Are you serious? You're still going to cry about Yusuf?"
  • The old sibling jealousy toward Yusuf (AS) appears to reignite: their entire crime years earlier was committed so that Yusuf would disappear and their father's heart would empty out for them alone.
  • Even now, after all these years, Yusuf still seems to occupy first place in their father's heart — "we are still number two" — and that realization angers them all over again.

The twisted logic of "loving" abuse

"I'm Yelling Because I Love You"
  • Some people believe that hurting someone is itself a form of loving them — the same mentality some abusive people use to justify their harshness.
  • On this reading, the brothers are angry and frustrated, even though deep down they do want their father's love — they simply have that desire twisted.
  • Earlier in the sūrah, the brothers spoke ill of Yusuf without much provocation, falsely accusing him of theft, and referred to him and his brother with a sense of "otherness" rather than calling him their own brother.
  • This pattern shows it does not take much for the brothers to become aggravated at the mere memory of Yusuf.
04

Reconciling With Ayah 86 & the Teacher's View

Ayah 86 supports the angry reading

"I Only Complain to Allah"
  • In the very next ayah, Yaʿqūb (AS) responds: he is only taking his overwhelming grief to Allah as a complaint — he is not speaking to his sons at all.
  • This can be read as him drawing a boundary after a harsh exchange: "I know from Allah what you don't know, leave me be" — consistent with a father who has just been spoken to harshly and turns away again.
  • Whichever reading is taken for ayah 85, the following ayah (86) or ayah 87 then needs to be reconciled with it — a puzzle to be resolved in the following lessons.

Where the teacher leans

A Personal, Not Final, Judgment
  • While both readings deserve respect, the harsher, angrier reading is described as more convincing, because it fits the pattern already established for the brothers elsewhere in the sūrah.
  • It doesn't take much for their old resentment toward Yusuf (AS) to resurface, and their harshness toward their father earlier in the story supports this reading being consistent with their character.
05

"Wallahi" vs "Ta-Allahi": Grades of Oath

Two Arabic particles for "I swear"

Wow al-Qasam vs Ta al-Qasam
  • The letter "wow" (و) used for oaths is common in the Qur'an — many sūrahs open with it (e.g. "wal-'asr," "wal-fajr"), and it can attach to any noun someone swears by.
  • Far rarer is the letter "ta" (ت) used for an oath, as in "Ta-Allahi" — grammarians such as az-Zamakhshari note it carries an added sense of shock or overwhelming emotion.
  • "Ta" oaths can only be used with Allah's name — never with any other noun — making it the highest, most emphatic form of oath in the Arabic language.

Casual "Wallahi" vs. serious "Ta-Allahi"

Everyday Speech vs. Clenched Teeth
  • In everyday Arab speech, "Wallahi" is often used casually — like saying "I swear, I really want ice cream right now" — without carrying real weight or consequence if unfulfilled.
  • "Ta-Allahi," by contrast, is not used casually. It reflects a moment of clenched fists and gritted teeth, where someone means exactly what they say and is speaking under real, intense emotion.
  • Here, the brothers used "Ta-Allahi" — signaling this was not an offhand complaint but words spoken with genuine rage and conviction.
06

Ibrahim's "Ta-Allahi": A Precedent

A young Ibrahim (AS) and the idols

"I Will Make a Scheme Against Your Idols"
  • One of the rare Qur'anic examples of "Ta-Allahi" comes from a young Ibrahim (AS), disgusted by his people's devotion to idols of wood and stone.
  • His fury is palpable in the wording chosen — he doesn't use the casual "Wallahi" but the emphatic "Ta-Allahi" when vowing to deal with the idols, showing he meant serious business.
  • What followed shows he truly meant it — he acted on what he swore, unlike a casual, unfulfilled "Wallahi."
07

Allah Translating Hebrew Grief Into Arabic

The original conversation was not in Arabic

A Family of Hebrew Speakers
  • Yaʿqūb's (AS) family were Hebrew speakers, not Arabs — so the actual conversation recorded here took place in Hebrew, and the Qur'an presents Allah's Arabic translation of it.
  • When Allah translates, He translates the underlying emotion, not merely the literal words — the brothers may have said far more than what is captured, but the Arabic wording is enough to convey the full weight of their rage.
  • A more emotionally accurate rendering in modern English might use an exclamation like "I swear to God, come on!" rather than a flat "we swear to God" — to capture the intensity that a smooth, calming recitation of the Arabic can obscure for a non-Arabic listener.

The gap between sound and meaning

Calm Recitation, Raging Scene
  • Someone unfamiliar with Arabic can listen to this ayah recited beautifully and calmly, absorbed only in the pleasant phonetics, without sensing that the actual scene was one of real anger and confrontation.
  • The brothers were not standing calmly before their father speaking evenly — they were enraged, and the words reflect that intensity.
08

The Irony of Invoking Allah's Name

They were already bound by a sacred oath

The Binyamin Oath, Turned Around
  • When the brothers took Binyamin, Yaʿqūb (AS) had made them swear an oath by Allah's name specifically — hoping their awe of Allah would restrain them from wronging their brother, even if their hearts still held ill feeling.
  • It is ironic, then, that the very name they were bound by now appears again — this time invoked in their disobedience toward their father, in a moment of anger rather than restraint.

Adab with parents, and a father like no other

Yaʿqūb (AS) as the Qur'an's Model Parent
  • The Qur'an teaches a timeless principle: children should treat parents with the utmost excellence and never show frustration toward them, even when parents test their patience.
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) is not simply "a parent" — he is described in the Qur'an as a role model any parent can learn from, and Surah al-Baqarah records his final words to his sons on his deathbed, asking what they will worship after he is gone.
  • Because he is cited in the Qur'an as an example for all of humanity until Judgment Day, the sons' disrespect toward him is not a small or trivial matter.

Believers whose emotions overran their faith

A Spiritual Truth Shelved
  • The brothers were believers — followers of revelation and mentioners of Allah's name — not outsiders to faith, yet their jealousy and rage overtook their spiritual awareness in this moment.
  • When emotions run this high, the deeper spiritual truth inside a person can effectively be "shelved" and locked away, causing words to come out that contradict one's own faith.
  • Allah does not hold people accountable for oaths taken carelessly, but He does hold them accountable for what the heart truly means — and this outburst clearly came from the heart.
09

"Tafta" and "Tathkuru": Grammar of Grief

"Tafta": will not stop, will not cease

A Grammatical License Rooted in Emotion
  • "Tafta" comes from a defective verb family and normally functions as "la tafta" ("you will not stop"), similar to how English says "will you still be at it" or "won't you ever stop."
  • In highly emotional expression, especially in pre-Islamic poetry, speakers took the license to drop the negation particle while keeping the same intended meaning — a feature of emotional speech rather than formal grammar.
  • Emotional speech tends to be less formal, sometimes concise to the point of dropping expected words, and sometimes overly emphatic elsewhere — which is exactly the texture of this ayah.

"Tathkuru": remembering and mentioning

Heart and Tongue Together
  • The root behind "tathkuru" (zikr) carries a double meaning: remembering something in the heart, and mentioning it aloud with the tongue — the same duality found whenever "zikr" appears in the Qur'an.
  • Interestingly, in Hebrew the words for "remembrance" and "mention" are also expressed by the same root, paralleling the Arabic.
  • The brothers' accusation therefore lands on two fronts at once: Yaʿqūb (AS) is still feeling Yusuf in his heart and still speaking of him with his tongue — and to them, both are unacceptable.
10

Love Is Not a Plate of Food

Old insecurity resurfacing

"Where Is the Room for Our Feelings?"
  • The name Yusuf comes up for a second time — earlier, Yaʿqūb (AS) had already asked, somewhat pointedly, whether he could trust them with Binyamin the way he once trusted them with Yusuf.
  • The brothers aren't only reacting to what their father said, but to how he still feels — they resent that his heart is still occupied by Yusuf, as though there is no room left for them.
  • Years earlier they had tried to eliminate Yusuf believing this would redirect their father's love toward themselves.

A flawed model of love

Love Can Be Shared
  • The brothers' underlying assumption is that love is a finite quantity, like a plate of food — the more given to one person, the less left for another.
  • In reality love is not black and white or quantifiable in that way: a parent can love multiple children fully, and the love given to one is not identical to, nor a subtraction from, the love given to another.
  • In their minds, so long as Yaʿqūb (AS) loves Yusuf, something essential is missing from what they receive from him — the same old grievance resurfacing yet again.

Deep Dive: The Word "Harad"

A rare and layered root

Plaster, Dye, and a Desert Plant
  • The root of "harad" is uncommon in the Qur'an, and its layers of meaning are worth unpacking one by one before drawing them together.
  • It is used for a plaster-like paste (ancient plaster akin to modern cement) that sticks permanently once applied.
  • It is used for cloth that has been dyed in a way that the color will not come out.
  • It names a skinny, withering green desert plant (identified as a species of saltwort native to the Jordan valley) whose extract is used both to make a dye that won't wash out, and to make a soap capable of removing that same dye.
  • A common thread across these meanings: something that either sticks and won't let go, or that removes something stuck and won't let go — two opposite senses drawn from the same root.

The card-dealer connection

A Person Assigned to Deal the Arrows
  • A less obviously related meaning of the root describes a person assigned to deal out gambling arrows in pre-Islamic Arab gambling — comparable to a modern card dealer.
  • A speculative link offered here: gambling is closely tied to depression and near-death experience for those who lose (the majority), and societies with heavy gambling tend to see more debt, suicide, and violent crime — so "dealing" could be tied to dealing people toward ruin or death, paralleling how "harad" brings a person near death.

Imam ar-Razi's description of a "harid" person

Overwhelmed to the Point of Collapse
  • A "harid" is someone overwhelmed and overrun by sadness and longing together, to the point of a disease that brings them near death.
  • Such a person's body and mind visibly deteriorate — comparable to someone in intensive care who begins hallucinating or mistaking loved ones for other people.
  • Longing and grief can make a person incapable of clear thought, paralyzed, and skeletal from weight loss — becoming, in effect, unrecognizable, like a completely different person.
  • At its essence, "harad" describes the corruption of both body and mind brought on by sadness combined with love.

Bringing the meanings together

Sticking, Stripping, and Inciting
  • Arabic triliteral roots often carry a cluster of meanings that, in creative or anthropological ways, connect to one another — sometimes clearly, sometimes only through interpretation.
  • The word gives both the sense of something that sticks and won't let go, and something being scraped or pulled off (as soap removes dye) — from which also comes the meaning "to incite," as if pulling out someone's buried motivation the way soap pulls dye from cloth.
  • Applied to Yaʿqūb (AS): the brothers are describing him as visibly wasting away, losing his mind, and approaching the edge of death — all because of what he still feels for Yusuf (AS).

The Psychology of Guilt Turned Outward

Blaming the one who reminds you

"You Did This to Me"
  • Rather than feeling ashamed at even hearing Yusuf's name, given their role in what happened to him, the brothers instead turn the moment into their father's fault for reminding them.
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) was not even accusing them of anything — he had explicitly said he was only speaking to Allah — yet they respond as though he were attacking them.
  • A guilty conscience can be used constructively, to turn toward Allah in repentance, or it can be hijacked by whispering that one is actually the victim and has a right to push back at whoever triggered the guilt.

The script gets flipped

A Familiar Family Pattern
  • The sin the brothers committed against Yusuf effectively disappears from view, replaced by a self-image in which they are now the victims of the mention of Yusuf.
  • This mirrors a well-known dynamic: people who did wrong often become skilled at making others feel guilty, especially guilty for simply feeling sad — accusing the aggrieved party of lacking patience ("sabr") when in fact it is the wrongdoers avoiding accountability.
  • This is described as a powerful and common psychological pattern within families, where a wrongdoer, when confronted or even just reminded, goes on the offensive instead of reflecting.

Irony: "Learn From Those Who Died"

"Halikeen": dying a violent, ugly death

A Word Stronger Than "Mawt"
  • The word used for "perish" here implies a harsh or violent death, distinct from the more neutral word for ordinary death.
  • The brothers effectively tell their father: look at how many people have gone down this road and ended up destroyed — as if urging him to learn from the fate of others who let grief consume them.

An ironic instruction

Learn From Others, Not From Yourselves
  • The irony: the very people telling Yaʿqūb (AS) to learn from other people's mistakes are themselves unwilling to learn from their own past mistake regarding Yusuf.
  • Instead of reflecting on where their own jealousy came from, they redirect the lesson entirely onto their father's grief.
14

Why Allah Dedicated a Whole Ayah to This

A minor exchange, given major weight

Selective Qur'anic Storytelling
  • The Qur'an is famously selective — Surah Yusuf is often described as the pinnacle of storytelling in the Qur'an, focusing on major turning points: the journeys to and from Egypt, the taking and losing of brothers, and so on.
  • This particular exchange is not one of the story's major plot events; it's one conversation among many that must have taken place during a long journey, yet Allah chose to record it in detail.
  • No human being at the time recorded this outburst, no consequence followed it in worldly terms — yet Allah preserved it, translated it, and made it part of a revelation recited until Judgment Day.

Words are not "just family talk"

Nothing Is Too Small to Matter
  • The lesson drawn is that some things people consider trivial — "I said it because I was upset," "it's just a family fight" — are, in fact, taken very seriously by Allah.
  • Everything a person says will be recorded and brought forward on Judgment Day, down to the smallest and largest details, which is why seeking Allah's forgiveness for careless words matters.
15

A Mirror for Us — Not Just Them

Two sides of the same audience

Recipients and Perpetrators
  • Among any audience hearing this ayah, some will identify as having received this kind of treatment from others, and some will recognize themselves as having done this kind of thing to someone else.
  • The instinct to immediately think of others who've wronged us is natural, but the more searching reflection is whether we ourselves have been guilty of this without fully realizing it.
  • Human beings are complex, holding both good and bad within them, and people often gloss over their own wrongdoing while focusing on someone else's shortcomings, to avoid facing their own guilt.

Fear of facing one's own failure

Avoidance as Self-Protection
  • Sometimes defensiveness after wrongdoing isn't pure evil — it may be fear: confronting what one did honestly might lead to a painful reckoning with one's own character.
  • People sometimes avoid facing what they've done because doing so risks a devastating verdict against themselves.
16

Past Actions Don't Define You

Confronting wrongdoing without hopelessness

What You Did vs. Who You Are
  • Facing what one has done wrong does not mean concluding one is a fundamentally bad person — it means acknowledging a specific action, which is a very different thing.
  • If people were entirely defined by their past actions, tawbah (repentance) would have no purpose at all.
  • Firaun is cited as the Qur'an's extreme example — a man responsible for mass killing and blasphemy — yet Allah still described hope that he might turn around, showing that no one's past is treated as an unchangeable verdict except for Iblis, who has no hope left in himself.

A new day, a fresh start

"Alhamdulillah — Who Gave Us Life After Death"
  • Iblis wants people to believe that acknowledging past wrong is either something to hide and justify, or something that should lead to despair — both are described as his tricks.
  • True repentance means reforming, stopping the wrong, and making amends where possible — while accepting that others may or may not forgive, which does not determine one's present worth.
  • The daily reminder of waking each morning — thanking Allah for giving life after a kind of "death" in sleep — is offered as a symbol of the fresh start available to everyone each day.

Preview: Yaqub Draws a Boundary

Addressing the root, not the branches

A Father Who Sees Past the Words
  • Yaʿqūb (AS) appears to have deep insight into his sons — recognizing that their aggressiveness is likely masking a guilt they are unable to face or confront directly.
  • Rather than reacting only to what they said, he addresses what is behind it — the thinking and feeling that drive the behavior, not just the behavior itself.
  • The principle drawn out: when the root (the heart) is fixed, the whole plant (behavior) follows — chasing surface behavior alone rarely works.

Boundaries before help

Looking Ahead to Ayah 86
  • In the very next ayah, Yaʿqūb (AS) is described as first drawing a boundary for himself before he is able to help his sons.
  • The principle offered: a person who is being walked over is not in a position to help anyone else — a boundary must come first, and only then can guidance follow.
