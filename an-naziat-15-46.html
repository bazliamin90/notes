Mūsā, Firʿawn & the Two Abodes [Āyāt 15–46]

The story of Mūsā (AS) being sent to Firʿawn, his escalating denial and destruction, the proofs of creation, and the two paths that end in either Hellfire or Paradise — closing with the questioning about the Hour.

01

Has the Story of Mūsā Come to You?

Verse 15

هَلۡ أَتَىٰكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ

"Has there reached you the story of Moses?" — Sahih International

Rhyme as a marker of Qur'ānic discourse

Discourse structure
  • The verses recited leading into this passage were repeated on purpose, even though some had already been covered — just to draw attention again to the shared end-rhyme.
  • Words like al-kubrā, fa-takhshā, tazakkā, Mūsā, Ṭuwā, and ṭaghā all end in the same "-ā" sound, which phonetically signals that these verses belong to one continuous passage — the same discussion carrying on.
  • Further ahead, a new series of end-syllables will mark a new grouping of āyāt — effectively a new paragraph.
  • This organization of Qur'ānic discourse through sound and rhyme is a real structural feature of the text, but one that is very difficult to convey through translation alone.
  • The rhetorical question "has the story of Mūsā come to you?" is a device to revisit the story — the Messenger ﷺ already knows it; it isn't new information.
02

"Would You Purify Yourself?"

Verses 16–17

إِذۡ نَادَىٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ بِٱلۡوَادِ ٱلۡمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى ‎ ٱذۡهَبۡ إِلَىٰ فِرۡعَوۡنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ

"[Mention] when his Lord called to him in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā, 'Go to Pharaoh; indeed, he has transgressed.'" — Sahih International

Verses 18–19

فَقُلۡ هَل لَّكَ إِلَىٰٓ أَن تَزَكَّىٰ وَأَهۡدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَتَخۡشَىٰ

"And say to him, 'Would you [be willing to] purify yourself, and let me guide you to your Lord so you would fear [Him]?'" — Sahih International

Only the relevant slice of the story

Selective narration
  • The Qur'ān never narrates a story in full — only the portion of Mūsā's (AS) story relevant to the surrounding discussion is cited here.
  • Mūsā's (AS) story recurs in over seventy places across the Qur'ān; every time, only the part relevant to that surah's discourse is brought up.
  • The ف at the start of verse 18 acts as a fā sababiyyah / fā ta'kīd, tightly connecting the command "go to Firʿawn" with what follows.
  • تَزَكَّىٰ is a contracted form of تَتَزَكَّىٰ (two "tā" letters merged into one) — a recognized Qur'ānic pattern (compare يَتَذَكَّرُونَ vs. يَذَّكَّرُونَ).
    • Though grammatically synonymous in ordinary usage, the Qur'ān treats the full and the contracted forms as carrying two different meanings.
    • The contracted form here conveys "even a little bit" of inclination to purify — not full or complete purification, just any small opening.
  • Classical commentary (e.g. Tafsīr Mudrik) notes that Firʿawn's worst rebellion was that he felt no guilt whatsoever — not even the slightest self-doubt — the same "Firʿawnic" mindset the Quraysh were slipping into.
  • وَأَهۡدِيَكَ ("and I guide you") is grammatically tied (manṣūb, due to the preceding أَنْ) to the earlier clause, not a separate sentence.
  • In the Fātiḥah, اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ omits إِلَىٰ, so the real meaning is "guide us to and along the straight path" — both knowledge and ongoing support, not just directions.
  • Mūsā's (AS) offer to Firʿawn instead uses إِلَىٰ ("guide you to your Lord") — offering only knowledge/direction, because guiding someone all the way along the path is something only Allah can do.
  • The word used for "Lord" here is رَبّ (Rabb), not "Allah" or "Ar-Raḥmān" — tying directly into the surah's central theme of Rubūbiyyah (Lordship), and continuing the closing theme of Sūrah An-Naba (رَبِّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ... الرَّحْمَٰنِ).
  • خَشْيَة (the fear used here) specifically means fear of something magnificent or greater than oneself — distinct from خَوْف — uniquely appropriate for Firʿawn, who regarded nothing as greater than himself.
  • Lesson drawn: true knowledge leads to fear. This is echoed by the description of أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ in Āl ʿImrān, who are shown pleading fearfully with Allah.
03

The Ultimate Sign

Verse 20

فَأَرَىٰهُ ٱلۡأٓيَةَ ٱلۡكُبۡرَىٰ

"And he showed him the greatest sign." — Sahih International

Al-Āyah Al-Kubrā

"Al-Kubrā" · greatest
  • كُبۡرَىٰ is the feminine superlative of أَكْبَر — the same root heard in "Allāhu Akbar."
  • Mūsā (AS) was given multiple signs, but Firʿawn's own reaction shows which one terrified him most: when he hired magicians to compete, they specifically brought ropes and rods to imitate the staff turning into a snake — not the shining hand, the locusts, or any of the other nine signs.
  • The parallel drawn: for the Quraysh, the "greatest sign" shown to them over the years was the Qur'ān itself — its miraculous eloquence and overwhelming rhetorical power.
  • Despite witnessing the miracle firsthand, nothing inside Firʿawn responded with any inclination toward goodness.
04

He Denied and Disobeyed

Verse 21

فَكَذَّبَ وَعَصَىٰ

"But he denied and disobeyed." — Sahih International

An object left out on purpose

"Kadhdhaba" · "ʿAṣā"
  • كَذَّبَ ("denied") is normally used with an object (e.g. كَذَّبَ بِآيَاتِهِ), but here no object is stated at all.
  • Leaving the object out implies Firʿawn lied against everything at once: against Mūsā (AS), against the sign itself, and against his own conscience.
  • Parallel to the Quraysh: those who see a great sign and still have some inkling of truth in them don't respond this way — only someone utterly devoid of goodness resorts to slandering the Messenger ﷺ.
  • Three distinct categories of disobedience appear across the Qur'ān:
    • عِصْيَان (used here) — refusing to do something one already knows to be good.
    • فِسْق — abandoning a path one previously walked rightly; repeated wrongdoing despite knowing better.
    • فُجُور — open, brazen, shameless disobedience, done proudly and publicly (echoed in Nūḥ's (AS) complaint describing offspring as "فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا").
  • In this surah, the Quraysh's own تَكْذِيب shows up earlier in their sarcastic remarks questioning resurrection and mocking it as a "losing return."
05

Turned Away in Haste

Verse 22

ثُمَّ أَدۡبَرَ يَسۡعَىٰ

"Then he turned his back, striving [against it]." — Sahih International

Pacing back and forth in restless desperation

"Yasʿā" · pacing
  • أَدۡبَرَ يَسۡعَىٰ pictures Firʿawn pacing back and forth — سَعْي is faster than walking but slower than running.
  • The image: Firʿawn up in his palace, restless, as Mūsā's (AS) message spreads further and cannot be stopped no matter how hard he tries to suppress it.
  • إِدْبَار is described as a lesser, hastier version of تَدْبِير (careful, deliberate planning) — Firʿawn is improvising under pressure, not calmly strategizing.
  • His escalating, contradictory rhetoric against Mūsā (AS) is traced across other parts of the Qur'ān, each accusation replacing the last as none of them worked:
    • First calling him مَجْنُون — crazy, possessed.
    • Then سَاحِر — a magician.
    • Then a threat that he wants to expel the people from their land.
    • Finally claiming to be the very one who "taught" all the magicians.

"I Am Your Lord, the Highest"

Verses 23–24

فَحَشَرَ فَنَادَىٰ فَقَالَ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلۡأَعۡلَىٰ

"And he gathered [his people] and called out, and said, 'I am your most exalted lord.'" — Sahih International

Herding people like animals into a forced address

"Ḥashara" vs. "Jamaʿa"
  • حَشَرَ ("gathered," used for herding animals — e.g. wild beasts on the Day of Judgment) is distinct from جَمَعَ (a simple, neutral gathering).
  • Firʿawn didn't just call people together — he forcibly herded them, since they had been at home privately discussing Mūsā's (AS) message.
  • He needed to force a public confrontation and reassert the "official state religion."
  • أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ ("I am your most exalted lord") — the basic claim of supreme rulership already existed as state propaganda, but adding "الأعلى" (the highest) implies open competition with another claimed Lord — a sign of desperation, not confidence.
  • The dialogue chain with Mūsā (AS), traced across other surahs, shows Mūsā (AS) repeatedly returning to the word "Rabb" no matter how Firʿawn tries to deflect:
    • Lord of the worlds → Lord of the heavens and earth → Lord of the east and west → Lord of the earliest forefathers.
  • A parallel passage (Sūrah Az-Zukhruf) shows Firʿawn publicly boasting about his rivers and kingdom, and mocking Mūsā's (AS) speech — outwardly arrogant, but actually revealing that his grip was weakening enough that he now had to justify himself in public.
  • The underlying point: a real political and spiritual shift was underway — fear of Firʿawn was subsiding as belief in a true Lord spread among the people.

Seized as a Lesson

Verses 25–26

فَأَخَذَهُ ٱللَّهُ نَكَالَ ٱلۡأٓخِرَةِ وَٱلۡأُولَىٰٓ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبۡرَةٗ لِّمَن يَخۡشَىٰٓ

"So Allah seized him in exemplary punishment for the last and the first [transgression]. Indeed in that is a lesson for whoever fears [Allah]." — Sahih International

Making a deterrent out of the tyrant himself

"Nakāl" · "ʿIbrah"
  • نَكَال historically refers to a chain or anklet used to restrict a prisoner's movement, or force prisoners to move together — also used before executions, as a public deterrent to instill fear in onlookers.
  • Ironically, Allah made Firʿawn himself into a نَكَال — a warning "for the last and the first" — reversing the exact method Firʿawn used to threaten others (he had once threatened to cut off hands and feet as "an example").
  • This serves two purposes at once: a stern warning to the Quraysh who doubted the Hereafter, and reassurance to the Messenger ﷺ that Allah has already dealt with far worse tyrants than them.
  • عِبْرَة ("lesson") comes from عُبُور — "to cross" — used for crossing a river, or for a tear crossing/falling from the eye.
    • This implies both deep sorrow and a "crossing" from falsehood into truth.
  • The lesson is conditional: it is only available to those capable of خَشْيَة — fear of something greater than themselves. Without that fear, no such "crossing" or realization happens.
  • This closes the third major passage of the surah (following the opening oaths, the resurrection passage, and now the Mūsā narrative); a shift in end-rhyme signals the start of a new, fourth passage next.

Creation of the Heavens and Earth

Verses 27–28

ءَأَنتُمۡ أَشَدُّ خَلۡقًا أَمِ ٱلسَّمَآءُۚ بَنَىٰهَا رَفَعَ سَمۡكَهَا فَسَوَّىٰهَا

"Are you a more difficult creation, or is the heaven? He constructed it. He raised its ceiling and proportioned it." — Sahih International

Verses 29–30

وَأَغۡطَشَ لَيۡلَهَا وَأَخۡرَجَ ضُحَىٰهَا وَٱلۡأَرۡضَ بَعۡدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَىٰهَآ

"And He darkened its night and extracted its brightness. And after that He spread the earth." — Sahih International

Verses 31–33

أَخۡرَجَ مِنۡهَا مَآءَهَا وَمَرۡعَىٰهَا وَٱلۡجِبَالَ أَرۡسَىٰهَا مَتَٰعٗا لَّكُمۡ وَلِأَنۡعَٰمِكُمۡ

"He extracted from it its water and its pasture, and the mountains He set firmly, as provision for you and your grazing livestock." — Sahih International

A reversed sequence and a seamless construction

Cosmic proofs
  • Allah poses a rhetorical challenge: is humanity harder to create, or is the heaven that He built? شَدِيد here conveys difficulty/intensity, not merely physical strength.
  • Unlike Sūrah An-Naba (which mentions the earth before the sky), this surah reverses the sequence — sky first, then earth — a deliberate compositional pattern across different surahs.
  • بِنَاء ("construction") implies interlocking pieces, like bricks or walls meshing together — the sky's construction is described as seamless.
  • سَمَاء is not limited to "the sky" — it refers to everything above the earth: the stars, the galaxies, all of creation above the planets.
  • رَفَعَ سَمْكَهَا ("raised its ceiling") — سَمْك refers to the highest point of a structure (also used for the highest hump of a camel). The height of the sky's "roof" is so extreme it's beyond human sight — paralleled elsewhere with "do you see any crack?" (Sūrah Al-Mulk).
  • فَسَوَّىٰهَا — He balanced, leveled, and perfected it.
  • Night and day are deliberately contrasted:
    • أَغْطَشَ describes darkness so intense that things become indistinguishable — you can't tell one thing from another — figuratively echoing the confusion of pre-revelation ignorance (which god, which fate).
    • ضُحَى (bright morning) is then drawn out of that darkness, symbolizing the clarity revelation brings.
    • Other Qur'ānic words for phases of night (عَسْعَسَ, وَسَقَ) each carry distinct shades of meaning, several concentrated in Juzʾ ʿAmma.
  • دَحَاهَا ("spread it") classically means to smoothen and spread out.
    • Some have connected the root (used for where an ostrich lays its egg) to an oval/round shape as a modern "science parallel," but the lecturer cautions against forcing scientific readings onto language rather than staying true to the classical meaning.
    • The spreading of the earth may also remind the disbelievers of the earlier-mentioned flat land (السَّاهِرَة) where they will eventually stand for judgment.
  • أَرْسَاهَا ("anchored," used for mountains) is the same root used for anchoring a ship or pegging something into the ground — mountains are immovable, unlike any man-made building that can be demolished. The same root recurs later regarding the Hour ("when will it be anchored/arrive?").
  • مَتَاعًا — "provision," from a root meaning "to use" (not necessarily to enjoy); historically even used for a scrubbing rag.
    • This defines worldly life (Dunyā) as something meant to be used for a higher purpose, not indulged in — directly relevant to the surah's core concern about preferring worldly pleasure over the Hereafter.

The Overwhelming Calamity

Verses 34–36

فَإِذَا جَآءَتِ ٱلطَّآمَّةُ ٱلۡكُبۡرَىٰ يَوۡمَ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلۡإِنسَٰنُ مَا سَعَىٰ وَبُرِّزَتِ ٱلۡجَحِيمُ لِمَن يَرَىٰ

"But when there comes the greatest catastrophe — the Day when man will remember what he strove for — and Hellfire will be exposed for [all] to see." — Sahih International

A grand, sudden, all-encompassing arrival

"Aṭ-Ṭāmmah Al-Kubrā"
  • جَاءَتْ (used here) conveys a grander, more severe "arrival" than أَتَتْ (a simpler word for "came") — intensified further by إِذَا, meaning "all of a sudden."
  • الطَّامَّة الْكُبْرَىٰ comes from طَمَّ — to completely fill something up (e.g. filling a well entirely with dirt) — describing an all-encompassing calamity with no escape in any direction.
  • يَتَذَكَّرُ (the uncontracted form) conveys thorough, complete recollection — every last deed remembered — unlike the contracted لِيَذَّكَّرُوا used elsewhere for only partial remembrance.
  • This links back to Firʿawn's pacing (سَعَىٰ, verse 22) — everyone will eventually recall everything they "rushed toward" in worldly pursuit, just as Firʿawn rushed to protect his rule.
  • The disbelievers' real crime in this surah is their mocking speech about resurrection — but their speech is shown to stem from deeper motives (what they were striving toward), which they will also be made to recall.
  • بُرِّزَتِ الْجَحِيمُ — Hellfire will be "brought forward," made visible for anyone who wishes to see — directly answering the skeptics' earlier taunt about being unable to visualize resurrection.
  • جَحِيم is linked to a root (جَحَمَ) describing a lion staring hungrily, or a blaze so intense it looks like it is staring back — a mutual, menacing "looking" between the fire and those who see it.

Two Paths, Two Abodes

Verses 37–39

فَأَمَّا مَن طَغَىٰ وَءَاثَرَ ٱلۡحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنۡيَا فَإِنَّ ٱلۡجَحِيمَ هِيَ ٱلۡمَأۡوَىٰ

"Then as for he who transgressed and preferred the worldly life, then indeed, Hellfire is the refuge." — Sahih International

Verses 40–41

وَأَمَّا مَنۡ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفۡسَ عَنِ ٱلۡهَوَىٰ فَإِنَّ ٱلۡجَنَّةَ هِيَ ٱلۡمَأۡوَىٰ

"But as for he who feared the standing before his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, then indeed, Paradise is the refuge." — Sahih International

A "refuge" that offers no refuge

The transgressor
  • طَغَىٰ directly echoes the earlier description of Firʿawn (verse 17), making clear this applies to any rebellious person, not only Firʿawn.
  • ءَاثَرَ ("gave preference to") can be used positively elsewhere (preferring others over oneself), but here it identifies the true root of rebellion: giving worldly life priority over the Hereafter.
    • The core problem is not disbelief itself, but misplaced preference — giving more weight, more priority, to this life.
  • الْمَأْوَىٰ ("refuge/shelter," from أَوَى, to find safety from danger) is used sarcastically for Hellfire — a "refuge" that is itself the very danger, mirroring the Quraysh's own earlier sarcasm about the Hereafter.

Fighting the nafs as an outside enemy

The God-fearing
  • For the one who feared standing before their Lord: خَوْف (a manifest, stronger fear) is used, distinct from the milder خَشْيَة Mūsā (AS) merely offered as a possibility to Firʿawn.
  • This fear is of the "standing" itself — زَمَان and مَكَان, the time and place of that confrontation before the Lord.
  • Allah says he restrained النَّفۡسَ — not "his own self" (نَفۡسَهُ) — framing the nafs as an external adversary to be actively fought, not simply one's own identity (echoing إِنَّ النَّفْسَ لَأَمَّارَةٌ بِالسُّوءِ).
  • الْهَوَىٰ — vain, empty desire that feels powerful in the moment but proves worthless once it passes.
  • For this person, الْجَنَّة is the true الْمَأْوَىٰ — a direct contrast to the Hellfire-as-refuge of the transgressor.
  • The lecturer notes that this surah's core theme is not belief vs. disbelief in the Hereafter per se, but preference for Dunyā over Ākhirah — a disease that can also afflict believing Muslims who acknowledge the Hereafter but treat it casually.

Questions About the Hour

Verses 42–44

يَسۡـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرۡسَىٰهَا فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكۡرَىٰهَآ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَىٰهَآ

"They ask you, [O Muhammad], about the Hour: when is its arrival? In what [position] are you that you should mention it? To your Lord is its finality." — Sahih International

Verses 45–46

إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخۡشَىٰهَا كَأَنَّهُمۡ يَوۡمَ يَرَوۡنَهَا لَمۡ يَلۡبَثُوٓاْ إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوۡ ضُحَىٰهَا

"You are only a warner for those who fear it. It will be, on the Day they see it, as though they had not remained [in the world] except for an afternoon or a morning thereof." — Sahih International

A limited role, and a final rhyme with the opening

"Munʿdhir" · warner
  • The disbelievers ask, sarcastically, "when will the Hour be anchored/arrive?" — reusing the same root أَرْسَى used earlier for the mountains, mocking the idea that something as vast as the Hour could ever "settle" the way mountains are anchored.
  • Allah redirects the question to the Messenger ﷺ: "In what capacity do you have to speak of it?" — not asking whether he discusses the Hour, but clarifying that his role is limited to warning that it is coming, not specifying dates or times.
  • إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَاهَا — full and complete knowledge of the Hour, from its beginning to its conclusion, belongs exclusively to Allah.
  • إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرٌ — the Messenger's ﷺ role is solely to warn those capable of خَشْيَة, echoing the same root used throughout the surah (verses 19, 26, 40, and here again in 45).
  • On the Day it is witnessed, the entirety of worldly life will feel to people like it lasted only a single afternoon (عَشِيَّة) or morning (ضُحَى) — a striking contrast in scale.
  • The surah's ending is deliberately tied back to its opening: the Hour was first introduced early on (يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الرَّاجِفَةُ ‎ تَتْبَعُهَا الرَّادِفَةُ — the two quaking blasts) and is revisited again at the very end, forming one cohesive, unified discourse across the whole surah.
